IT Policies and Compliance

An IT policy is a formal, management-approved rule for how technology may be used and managed. Compliance means you can show that you follow your own policies and the laws, regulations and standards that apply to you. Documentation is how you show it.

Policies, standards, procedures and guidelines

These four words are often mixed up, but they form a clear hierarchy. Each level is more specific than the one above it, and each must agree with the level above.

LevelMandatory?Example: passwords
PolicyYes“All accounts must be protected by strong authentication.”
StandardYes“Passwords must be at least 12 characters. MFA is required for administrator and remote access.”
ProcedureYes“How to reset a user's password”, a step-by-step SOP.
GuidelineNo“Consider a passphrase of four or more random words.”

Core IT policies

Most organizations keep a set of core IT policies. Open each one to see what it covers.

Acceptable Use Policy (AUP)

Purpose: Defines what users may and may not do with company computers, networks, email and internet access.

  • Company systems are for business use; limited personal use may be allowed.
  • No illegal content, piracy, harassment or attempts to bypass security controls.
  • Users are told that activity may be monitored. This matters for privacy and ethics.
  • Every user signs or electronically accepts the AUP before getting an account.
Password & Authentication Policy

Purpose: Protects accounts from unauthorized access.

  • Minimum length (e.g. 12+ characters); long passphrases encouraged.
  • MFA for administrators, remote access and email.
  • Passwords are changed when compromise is suspected, rather than on a fixed schedule (current NIST SP 800-63B guidance).
  • Never share passwords; store them only in an approved password manager.

Access Control Policy

Purpose: Makes sure people only have the access their job requires.

  • Least privilege and need-to-know.
  • Access is requested, approved and recorded. See the user provisioning SOP.
  • Administrators use a separate admin account for privileged work.
  • Access is reviewed regularly (e.g. quarterly) and removed on the day someone leaves.
Backup & Recovery Policy

Purpose: Makes sure data can be recovered after deletion, failure, ransomware or disaster.

  • Follow the 3-2-1 rule: 3 copies, on 2 different media, 1 off-site.
  • Define RPO (how much data you can afford to lose) and RTO (how fast you must recover).
  • Test restores regularly and record the result. See the backup runbook.
Patch & Vulnerability Management Policy

Purpose: Keeps systems protected against known vulnerabilities.

  • Patch timeframes by severity (e.g. critical within 7 days, others within 30 days).
  • Test patches before production; schedule maintenance windows.
  • Track exceptions for systems that cannot be patched.
Change Management Policy

Purpose: Prevents outages caused by unplanned or untested changes.

  • Production changes need an approved change request with a test plan and a backout plan.
  • Major changes are reviewed by a CAB.
  • Emergency changes are documented afterwards. See the change request sample.
Incident Response Policy

Purpose: Defines how security and service incidents are reported, handled and learned from.

  • Who to contact, severity levels and escalation paths.
  • Phases: preparation → detection → containment → eradication → recovery → lessons learned.
  • Legal duties such as personal data breach notification. See the incident report sample.
Data Classification & Retention Policy

Purpose: Defines how sensitive each type of data is, how long to keep it and how to dispose of it.

  • Classes such as Public, Internal, Confidential and Restricted.
  • Retention periods (e.g. logs for 1 year, financial records as required by law).
  • Secure disposal: wipe or destroy media, for example following NIST SP 800-88.
Remote Work & BYOD Policy

Purpose: Sets rules for working outside the office and for personal devices (Bring Your Own Device).

  • VPN required for internal systems; no public Wi-Fi without VPN.
  • Devices must have screen lock, updates and disk encryption.
  • The company can remotely wipe company data from a lost device.

The policy lifecycle

  1. Identify the needA new law, an audit finding, an incident or a new technology shows that a rule is needed.
  2. DraftThe policy owner writes the policy using the standard template.
  3. ReviewIT, Information Security, HR and Legal review it for accuracy, fairness and legality.
  4. ApproveSenior management signs it off. A policy without management approval has no authority.
  5. Communicate & trainPublish it where staff can find it, train them, and collect acknowledgments.
  6. Enforce & monitorApply it consistently, using technical controls where possible.
  7. ReviewReview at least once a year, or sooner if laws, systems or risks change.

What is compliance?

Compliance means meeting the requirements that apply to your organization, and being able to prove it. Requirements come from four places:

  • Laws and regulations, e.g. the Data Privacy Act of 2012, which are mandatory.
  • Contracts, e.g. PCI DSS, if you accept credit cards.
  • Voluntary standards, e.g. ISO/IEC 27001 certification to win customer trust.
  • Internal policies, the organization's own rules.

Philippine laws every sysadmin should know

Data Privacy Act of 2012 (Republic Act No. 10173)

The main Philippine law protecting personal information. It is enforced by the National Privacy Commission (NPC).

  • Processing must follow the principles of transparency, legitimate purpose and proportionality.
  • Organizations must implement organizational, physical and technical security measures. Sysadmins implement most of the technical ones: access control, encryption, logging, backups and secure disposal.
  • Organizations must designate a Data Protection Officer (DPO).
  • Data subjects have rights, including the right to be informed, to access, to object, to correct and to erasure or blocking of their data.
  • Under NPC rules, serious personal data breaches must be reported to the NPC and affected people within 72 hours of discovery.

Why this matters to sysadmins

The Act sets criminal penalties, including imprisonment and fines that can reach millions of pesos. They cover acts such as unauthorized processing, access due to negligence, and concealing a security breach. Sysadmins usually discover breaches first, so knowing the reporting procedure is part of the job.

Cybercrime Prevention Act of 2012 (Republic Act No. 10175)

Defines and penalizes cybercrimes, including:

  • Illegal access: accessing a computer system without right. Administrators must stay within their authorization.
  • Illegal interception of non-public data transmissions.
  • Data interference and system interference: altering, deleting or hindering data or systems without right.
  • Misuse of devices: possessing or distributing tools or passwords meant for committing these offenses.

It also requires service providers to preserve traffic data and subscriber information for a minimum period, which affects log retention policies.

Electronic Commerce Act of 2000 (Republic Act No. 8792)

Gives electronic documents and signatures legal recognition. This is why properly controlled electronic records (logs, approvals, e-signed policies) can serve as evidence.

How documentation proves compliance

Auditors have a saying: “If it isn't documented, it didn't happen.” A policy only counts if you can produce evidence that it is followed.

Preparing for an IT audit

An audit is an independent check that controls are designed well and actually work. Internal audits are done by the organization's own auditors; external audits by regulators, customers or certification bodies.

  1. Know the scope and criteriaWhich systems, which period, and against which standard or law?
  2. Gather evidenceCollect current policies, procedures, logs and records in one place.
  3. Self-assessRun a gap analysis, for example with the checklist below.
  4. Fix the gapsWrite a corrective action plan with owners and due dates.
  5. During the auditAnswer honestly, show the evidence, and never guess or invent records.
  6. After the auditAddress the findings, document corrective actions and update your policies.

Consequences of non-compliance

  • Legal: fines, lawsuits and, under the Data Privacy Act and Cybercrime Prevention Act, possible imprisonment.
  • Financial: penalties, incident costs and lost business.
  • Certification: loss of ISO certification or the right to process card payments.
  • Reputation: customers and partners lose trust.
  • Personal: disciplinary action against the individuals involved.

Key takeaways

  • Policy (what/why) → Standard (specific rules) → Procedure (how) → Guideline (advice).
  • Every organization needs core policies: AUP, passwords, access, backup, patching, change, incident, data retention and remote work.
  • Policies follow a lifecycle and must be approved, communicated, enforced and reviewed.
  • In the Philippines, the Data Privacy Act (RA 10173) and Cybercrime Prevention Act (RA 10175) apply directly to sysadmin work.
  • Compliance is proven with documentation and records. If it isn't documented, it didn't happen.
